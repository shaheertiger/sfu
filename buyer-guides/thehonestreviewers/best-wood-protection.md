For most outdoor wood protection, the best single product is a pigmented, penetrating oil stain and sealer like **Ready Seal Exterior Stain and Sealer**. It soaks in instead of forming a film, so it blocks water and UV without peeling, goes on without lap marks, and can be refreshed later with a cleaning and another coat.

But "wood protection" covers several jobs. A clear repellent on a new deck, solid stain on gray siding, varnish on a chair and preservative on the cut end of a 4x4 are not interchangeable, and using the wrong one is a common reason outdoor wood fails early. Below is the best product for each job, then how to choose.

## How we picked

We sorted products by job, then chose the best widely available option for each. ## 1. Ready Seal Exterior Stain and Sealer: Best overall

Ready Seal is an oil stain that penetrates rather than building a film. Films on horizontal wood eventually crack and peel; a penetrating stain just wears thinner, so maintenance is wash, dry, recoat.

Its selling point is forgiveness: Ready Seal says it needs no back-brushing and no wet edge, so you can spray, roll or brush without lap marks. The pigment gives UV protection a clear sealer can't. One quirk: the color keeps developing for about two weeks, so don't judge it on day one.

**Key specs**
- Type: oil-based, semi-transparent stain and sealer in one
- Coverage: roughly 125-175 sq ft per gallon on most decks (more on smooth, dense wood, less on rough-sawn)
- Dry to the touch in under an hour per listings; full color in about 14 days
- Sizes: 1 and 5 gallon, plus aerosol cans for touch-ups

**Pros**
- Very hard to get lap marks or streaks
- Fades instead of peeling, so recoats are simple
- Pigmented colors give meaningful UV protection

**Cons**
- Needs a recoat on sunny decks more often than on fences, often every 1-3 years
- Prior non-Ready Seal semi-transparent or solid stains must be removed first
- Oil cleanup and solvent smell

Skip it if: your wood is already covered in a peeling solid stain you don't want to strip.

## 2. Thompson's WaterSeal Advanced Natural Wood Protector: Best clear water repellent

A clear water repellent is the lightest form of protection. It keeps water out, which cuts down on swelling, checking and cracking. Thompson's Advanced Natural Wood Protector is a water-based, one-coat product that Sherwin-Williams (Thompson's parent company) says can go on damp or dry wood, including new pressure-treated lumber right away.

The catch is UV: with no pigment, the sun will still gray the wood. Treat it as a one-year product on decks. Our look at [whether Thompson's Water Seal works](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/does-thompsons-water-seal-work/) goes deeper.

**Key specs**
- Type: water-based clear water repellent
- One coat; can be applied to damp or dry wood (per manufacturer)
- Coverage: varies with wood porosity; plan on roughly 200-300 sq ft per gallon

**Pros**
- Lets you protect new pressure-treated wood without waiting months
- Keeps the natural wood color at first
- Low cost, easy soap-and-water cleanup

**Cons**
- Wood will still gray in sun
- Short service life on horizontal surfaces, usually about a year
- Clear finishes show wear unevenly in high-traffic paths

Skip it if: your wood gets several hours of direct sun a day and you want it to keep its color.

## 3. Cabot Australian Timber Oil: Best for hardwoods and an oiled look

Cabot Australian Timber Oil is a translucent penetrating oil and a long-standing choice for dense woods like ipe and mahogany, plus cedar and redwood. Dense hardwoods resist typical deck stains, which sit on the surface and turn sticky; a thin, penetrating oil is the better match.

The tradeoff is time: Cabot lists a 24-48 hour dry time and a 50-90°F application window. The standard formula isn't sold in some states due to VOC rules; a Low VOC version covers less.

**Key specs**
- Type: oil-based, translucent penetrating finish
- Coverage: 250-600 sq ft per gallon (standard); 250-350 (Low VOC)
- Dry time: 24-48 hours; apply at 50-90°F

**Pros**
- Excellent penetration on dense hardwoods
- Rich, warm colors that show the grain
- Wears by fading, not peeling

**Cons**
- Long dry time keeps the deck out of use for a day or two
- Translucent finish needs more frequent recoats in full sun than a heavier stain
- VOC limits affect which version you can buy

Skip it if: you need the deck back in use the same afternoon.

## 4. Behr Premium Solid Color Waterproofing Stain & Sealer: Best solid stain

Solid stains hide the grain completely, which makes them the right tool for old, gray, patched or mismatched wood. All that pigment also makes them the most UV-resistant stain type.

Behr's 100% acrylic version is stocked at Home Depot and tints to a huge range of colors. Behr claims up to 10 years on decks and 25 on fences and siding, under a limited warranty tied to proper prep. Read those numbers with care: on decks, any solid stain can peel in high-traffic or wet areas.

**Key specs**
- Type: 100% acrylic, water-based solid color stain
- Mfr claim: up to 10 years on decks, up to 25 years on fences and siding
- Soap-and-water cleanup; many tint colors

**Pros**
- Best UV and color retention of any stain type
- Hides weathering, patches and mixed boards
- Easy to find and match later

**Cons**
- Hides the wood grain entirely
- Can peel on decks, and peeling means scraping and sanding
- Hard to go back to a semi-transparent look without stripping

Skip it if: you want to see wood grain or you're finishing a brand-new deck.

## 5. Minwax Helmsman Spar Urethane: Best for outdoor furniture and trim

Furniture, doors, railing caps and trim are touched and seen up close, so a clear film finish makes sense. Spar urethane is formulated to stay more flexible than interior polyurethane, and Minwax says the current oil-based Helmsman contains more UV blockers than earlier versions.

It's not a deck finish: films wear through underfoot and get slippery. Even on furniture, a sun-exposed film eventually cracks, so scuff-sand and recoat before it fails. For oiled teak or other tropical hardwood furniture, see our [teak oil guide](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/best-teak-oil/) instead.

**Key specs**
- Type: oil-based spar urethane (gloss, semi-gloss and satin)
- Coverage: about 125 sq ft per quart; three coats recommended
- Recoat in about 4-6 hours depending on version; dry in 24 hours

**Pros**
- Hard, clear, glossy protection for furniture and trim
- Widely available in quarts, gallons and aerosol
- Easy maintenance if you recoat before it peels

**Cons**
- Ambers over time, especially on light woods
- Will crack and peel in full sun if neglected
- Wrong product for decks and fences

Skip it if: the wood is walked on or you don't want to maintain a film.

## 6. Copper-Green Wood Preservative: Best end-cut treatment for pressure-treated lumber

Pressure treatment often doesn't reach the core of a board, so every cut, notch or drilled hole exposes wood with little preservative. Industry guidance (AWPA Standard M4) calls for brushing those cuts with a field-applied preservative, typically copper naphthenate. We cover the bigger picture in [should you seal pressure-treated wood](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/should-you-seal-pressure-treated-wood/).

Copper-Green is a widely stocked, EPA-registered copper preservative labeled for above-ground and ground-contact exterior wood. It tints wood green (a brown version is also sold), fading over months in sun. AWPA guidance references 2% copper as metal, and the naphthenate percentage on a label is not the same thing, so check. Nisus QNAP 2 is sold explicitly as a 2% copper naphthenate end-cut treatment.

**Key specs**
- Type: copper-based wood preservative, solvent-borne
- Label coverage: about 150-300 sq ft per gallon by brush
- Exterior use only; labeled for ground contact

**Pros**
- Fixes the weakest spot in any pressure-treated build
- Cheap insurance for posts, joists and ledger cuts
- Can be stained over once dry (check label)

**Cons**
- Strong solvent odor; wear gloves and work outdoors
- Green color shows on visible cuts until it fades or is stained
- Messy; it's a brush-and-drip product

Skip it if: you aren't working with pressure-treated lumber or ground-contact wood.

## 7. Bora-Care: Best borate for rot and insect protection

Borates are the best wood preservative most homeowners can buy for rot fungi, termites, carpenter ants and wood-boring beetles. They diffuse into bare wood and stay there as long as it stays dry. Bora-Care is a liquid concentrate with a glycol carrier that penetrates deeper than powder borates. Tim-bor, a powder mixed with water, is the cheaper option for new construction and prevention.

Know the limits: borates are water-soluble and wash out of wood exposed to rain or soil. They suit sills, framing, log homes and similar wood that's protected or topcoated afterward. Bora-Care must go on bare, unfinished wood, and you can stain or seal over it once dry.

**Key specs**
- Type: EPA-registered borate (disodium octaborate tetrahydrate) concentrate
- Mix: 1:1 with water for most uses; label coverage varies by job
- Must be applied to bare wood

**Pros**
- Treats rot fungi and wood-destroying insects
- Low odor and low toxicity to people compared with oil preservatives
- Can be finished over afterward

**Cons**
- Leaches out if wood is left exposed to rain or soil
- Expensive per gallon
- Not a substitute for a water-repellent or stain topcoat

Skip it if: the wood is in ground contact or will stay bare and wet.

## How to choose wood protection

### 1. Match the product to the job

Most failures come from mismatches. Rough guide:
- **Decks:** penetrating semi-transparent oil stain. Films peel under foot traffic.
- **Fences and siding:** semi-transparent or solid stain. Vertical surfaces shed water and last roughly twice as long as decks. See our [fence stain picks](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/best-fence-stain/).
- **Furniture, doors, trim:** spar urethane or a furniture oil.
- **Cut ends and drilled holes in treated lumber:** copper naphthenate end-cut solution.
- **Rot or insect risk in protected wood:** borate.

### 2. Pigment is your UV protection

Sunlight breaks down lignin, the glue between wood fibers, at the surface, which turns wood gray and fuzzy. Pigment blocks UV; clear "UV inhibitors" only slow it. As a rule of thumb: clear repellent lasts about a year on a sunny deck, semi-transparent roughly 1-3 years, and solid stain longer on vertical surfaces. Our comparison of [deck stain vs sealer](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/deck-stain-vs-sealer/) breaks this down further.

### 3. Penetrating vs film-forming

Penetrating finishes (oils, semi-transparent stains, water repellents) soak in and fade gradually. Film-formers (solid stains, paint, spar urethane) sit on top and last longer when intact, but fail by cracking and peeling. Pick penetrating for anything you walk on.

### 4. Oil vs water-based

Oil-based products generally penetrate deeper and are more forgiving on rough or weathered wood. Water-based products dry faster, clean up with water and are easier to buy in low-VOC states, but can show lap marks in heat.

### 5. Prep matters more than brand

Stain won't soak into dirty, gray or mill-glazed wood. Clean, brighten with an oxalic acid brightener, and let it dry 24-48 hours. New pressure-treated lumber usually needs to dry until water soaks in rather than beading, unless the label says otherwise.

### 6. Check the weather window

Most stains want 50-90°F, no rain for 24-48 hours, and wood that isn't baking in direct sun.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Putting spar urethane or any film finish on a deck
- Skipping end-cut treatment on posts and joists
- Applying a clear sealer and expecting it to stop graying
- Over-applying penetrating stain; wipe puddles or it turns sticky
- Using borates on wood that stays wet and bare

## FAQ

### What is the best wood preservative for outdoor wood?

For rot and insects on wood that's protected from the weather, a borate like Bora-Care or Tim-bor is the best preservative a homeowner can apply. For cut ends of pressure-treated lumber and ground-contact wood, use a copper naphthenate solution. For general weather protection on decks and fences, a pigmented penetrating stain does more than a preservative alone.

### How do you protect outdoor wood from rain and sun?

Use a pigmented, penetrating stain: the oil or resin repels water and the pigment blocks UV. Apply thin coats on clean, dry wood, and recoat when water stops beading. Our [exterior wood sealer guide](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/best-exterior-wood-sealer/) covers more clear and tinted options.

### Is a water repellent for wood the same as a sealer?

Mostly, yes. Both terms describe clear or lightly tinted penetrating products that keep water out. Neither stops UV graying the way a stain does, and both usually need yearly reapplication on decks.

### What is the best exterior wood sealer for pressure-treated wood?

If you want to protect it immediately, a product labeled for damp, new treated wood, like Thompson's Advanced Natural Wood Protector, is the easy route. Once the wood has dried, a semi-transparent oil stain like Ready Seal gives longer protection. See our [pressure-treated deck stain picks](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/best-deck-stain-for-pressure-treated-wood/).

### How often should outdoor wood be resealed?

Clear repellents on decks usually need it yearly; semi-transparent stains roughly every 1-3 years on decks and less often on fences. When water soaks in instead of beading, it's time.
