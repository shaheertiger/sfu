The best dehumidifier for a cold basement for most homes is the **AlorAir Sentinel HDi65S**: it is rated to run down to about 34°F with automatic defrost, removes about 55 pints a day at standard AHAM conditions, and has a built-in condensate pump, for well under the price of most premium crawl-space units. If your basement stays above about 50°F and you want top efficiency, the **Santa Fe Compact70** is the step up. If it regularly drops into the low 40s or 30s, a desiccant unit is the more reliable tool.

Cold basements are hard on ordinary dehumidifiers. A compressor unit chills air below its dew point to pull water out, and in a 55°F room the coil can drop below freezing. Frost builds up, the unit spends more time defrosting, and real output falls well under the number on the box. You want a unit built for those conditions, sized for that loss, and set up to drain on its own.

If your basement is warm and damp in summer, our general guide to the [best dehumidifier for a basement](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/best-dehumidifier-for-basement/) covers more portable options.

## How to choose the best dehumidifier for a cold basement

### 1. Know your basement's real temperature
Log temperature and humidity with a cheap hygrometer for a week in winter and in summer. Rough guide:
- **Above 65°F:** almost any good portable works.
- **55°F to 65°F:** pick auto-defrost and expect output well below the rating. Efficient portables, Santa Fe or Aprilaire work.
- **45°F to 55°F:** crawl-space units rated into the 30s are the better bet; 41°F portables spend a lot of time defrosting.
- **Below 45°F:** consider desiccant, or ask whether you need dehumidification at all in winter.

Cold air holds little moisture, so a basement in January is often drier than you think. If humidity stays under about 50% in winter, switch the unit off; our guide on whether [you need a dehumidifier in winter](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/do-you-need-a-dehumidifier-in-winter/) explains more.

### 2. Read pint ratings by test condition
Since mid-2019, the DOE has rated portable dehumidifiers at 65°F and 60% RH. Before that, the test was 80°F and 60% RH. The same unit now gets a lower number: an old "70-pint" portable is roughly today's 50-pint unit. Crawl-space and whole-home units are often still advertised at AHAM's 80°F rating, so a "70-pint" Santa Fe and a "50-pint" Midea aren't on the same scale. Compare at the same condition when you can, such as the 65°F figure on ENERGY STAR listings.

### 3. Look for auto-defrost and a low rated minimum
Auto-defrost senses frost and pauses or reverses the cycle to melt it. Without it, the coil ices over and the unit draws power without removing water. Check the manual for the minimum operating temperature: common figures are 41°F for portables, 49-50°F for Santa Fe and Aprilaire, and around 34°F for AlorAir's Sentinel line.

### 4. LGR, standard compressor or desiccant
- **Standard compressor:** cheapest, efficient at 65°F and up, loses capacity fast as it gets colder.
- **LGR (low-grain refrigerant):** a compressor design that pre-cools air before it reaches the coil, so it can wring out more water when the air is cool or relatively dry.
- **Desiccant:** works near freezing, but capacity is modest and energy use per pint is higher in warm air.

### 5. Size it with a cold-weather margin
For a cool basement, size up a step from what a warm-basement chart says:
- Up to ~1,000 sq ft, moderately damp: a 35- to 50-pint (2019 rating) portable.
- 1,000-2,000 sq ft, or musty with visible condensation: a 50-pint portable at minimum, or a 55- to 70-pint (AHAM) crawl-space unit.
- Over 2,000 sq ft, or very wet: a 70- to 90-pint (AHAM) unit, or a ducted [whole-house dehumidifier](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/best-whole-house-dehumidifier/).

### 6. Plan the drain before you buy
A unit that fills a bucket every 12 hours will get switched off. Use a gravity drain to a floor drain or sump if the hose can run downhill, or buy a model with a built-in condensate pump to lift water to a sink or exterior line. Don't run a drain line outdoors where it can freeze.

### 7. Set the right target
Aim for 45-50% relative humidity. Staying under 60% keeps mold and dust mites in check; much below 40% wastes power. If you see mold already, deal with it first using our guide on [how to get rid of mold in a basement](https://www.thehonestreviewers.com/how-to-get-rid-of-mold-in-basement/).

### Mistakes to avoid
- Buying by the box's pint number without checking the test temperature.
- Putting a 41°F-rated portable in a basement that sits at 45°F and expecting full output.
- Setting humidity to 35% "to be safe." It raises your bill for little benefit.
- Using a dehumidifier to cover for water leaks. Fix grading, gutters and cracks first.

## FAQ

### What is the best dehumidifier for a cool basement?
Prioritize auto-defrost and a low rated minimum. The AlorAir Sentinel HDi65S suits colder unfinished spaces, the Santa Fe Compact70 is the efficient choice above about 50°F, and a 50-pint portable with a pump, like the Midea Cube, is enough in the 60s.

### Is there a dehumidifier that works below 65 degrees?
Yes. 65°F is the DOE test temperature, not a cutoff. Most portables are rated down to about 41°F, Santa Fe and Aprilaire to 49-50°F, and AlorAir's Sentinel line to about 34°F. Expect less water removal as it gets colder.

### Do I need a basement dehumidifier with auto defrost?
If your basement ever sits below about 65°F, yes. Without auto-defrost, frost builds up on the coil, airflow drops, and the unit can run for hours without removing water. Most current models include it, but confirm in the specs.

### What is a low temperature dehumidifier?
It's a unit designed to keep removing moisture in cool air, with a low rated minimum, strong auto-defrost, and sometimes an LGR or desiccant design. Desiccant units are the most cold-tolerant because there's no coil to freeze.
