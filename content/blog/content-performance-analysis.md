You've got a month of posts behind you and a pile of numbers in every app, and you still can't say what actually worked. Content performance analysis fixes that: measure each post against its goal, compare posts fairly with a simple score, and use what you learn to decide what to publish next.

![Content performance analysis in four steps: set goals, pick metrics, score each post, change the calendar](/images/blog/content-performance-analysis-hero-1200x675.webp)

It isn't obvious because every platform shows you dozens of metrics, and totals reward whatever got lucky with distribution. You don't need a data team or expensive software, just consistency: the same metrics, measured the same way, reviewed on a regular schedule.

This guide covers which metrics matter for each goal, a four-step scoring framework, a spreadsheet you can set up in an hour, how often to review, and how to turn findings into next month's calendar.

## Why Most Content Analysis Goes Wrong (Goals Before Metrics)

![Four content goals and what to measure for each: awareness, engagement, traffic and conversion](/images/blog/content-performance-analysis-goals-1200x675.webp)

Most of the numbers in an analytics screen don't matter for any given post. Before you open analytics, write down what the content is supposed to do.

| Goal | Typical content | What you care about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Educational videos, shareable graphics, trend formats | Reach, and how much came from people who don't follow you yet |
| Engagement | Questions, behind-the-scenes, community spotlights | Comments, replies, saves and shares relative to reach |
| Traffic | Posts pointing to a blog post, product page or sign-up form | Link clicks, click-through rate, and what visitors did next |
| Conversion | Posts meant to drive a sale, booking or lead | Conversions in your website analytics or store, usually through UTM-tagged links |

A single post can serve more than one goal, but give each post one primary goal. It makes scoring much simpler.

### What Most People Don't Know

Totals mislead. A post that reached 50,000 people and got 500 comments did less, proportionally, than a post that reached 2,000 and got 100. Rates tell you whether the content itself resonated; totals mostly tell you how the algorithm distributed it.

> **Key point:** Judge posts by rates against your own baseline, not by raw totals and not against someone else's benchmark.

## The 10 Metrics That Matter (and Which Goal Each One Serves)

Platform naming varies, so match these to the closest metric in each app's analytics.

| Metric | What it tells you | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reach | How many unique accounts saw the post | Awareness |
| Impressions / views | How many times it was shown or played | Awareness, video |
| Engagement rate | Interactions divided by reach (or impressions) | Engagement |
| Saves | People wanted to come back to it | Educational content |
| Shares / reposts | People found it worth passing on | Awareness, growth |
| Comments / replies | It sparked a conversation | Community |
| Link clicks and CTR | It drove people off-platform | Traffic |
| Average watch time / completion | People actually watched | Video |
| Follows from post | It turned viewers into followers | Growth |
| Conversions | It drove a sale, lead or sign-up | Revenue |

Pick two or three per goal and ignore the rest. Three rules keep the numbers honest:

- **Use rates, not just totals.** Calculate engagement rate, click-through rate and save rate so posts of different sizes can be compared.
- **Be consistent about the denominator.** Some teams calculate engagement rate on reach, others on impressions, others on followers. Any is fine if you use the same one every time. Write your definitions at the top of your spreadsheet so they don't drift.
- **Measure at a fixed point in time.** Pick a window, such as seven days after publishing, and record numbers then. If you can only check monthly, note the publish date so you can account for newer posts having less time.

If you haven't turned on analytics yet, start there. For Instagram, see [how to turn on Insights on Instagram](/blog/how-to-turn-on-insights-on-instagram).

## How to Score Every Post in 4 Steps (Free Framework)

![Four steps to score a post: set a median baseline, index against it, weight by goal, add it up](/images/blog/content-performance-analysis-scoring-1200x675.webp)

Raw metrics are hard to compare across goals and platforms. A score turns each post into one number you can sort by.

### Step 1: Set a Median Baseline for Each Metric

For each platform, calculate your median for the key metrics over the last 20 to 30 posts. Use the median, not the average, because one viral post won't distort it.

### Step 2: Index Each Post Against the Baseline

Divide each post's metric by the baseline. A post with a save rate twice your median gets 2.0 for that metric. A post at half the median gets 0.5.

### Step 3: Weight the Metrics by the Post's Goal

| Goal | Example metric weights |
| --- | --- |
| Awareness | Reach 50%, shares 30%, follows 20% |
| Engagement | Engagement rate 40%, comments 30%, saves 30% |
| Traffic | CTR 60%, link clicks 40% |
| Conversion | Conversions 70%, CTR 30% |

### Step 4: Add It Up and Read the Score

Multiply each indexed metric by its weight and add them up.

| Score | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Above 1.3 | A clear win | Repeat the format, topic or hook |
| Around 1.0 | Your typical level | Keep, and look for small tweaks |
| Below 0.7 | A miss | Work out why before you repeat it |

These thresholds are starting points. Adjust them once you've seen a few months of your own data.

> **Pro tip:** The step people get wrong is Step 1. If you compare against an average that includes one viral hit, every normal post looks like a failure. Use the median, and rebuild it each month.

The point isn't mathematical precision. It's that every post gets judged the same way, so you stop arguing from gut feeling.

## Set Up the Spreadsheet in an Hour (Columns and Tags)

A plain spreadsheet works for most teams. Use one row per post and these columns:

- **Date and time posted**
- **Platform**
- **Format** (short video, carousel, single image, text, link post, live)
- **Topic or content pillar**
- **Hook type** (question, bold claim, how-to, story, list)
- **Primary goal**
- **Raw metrics** (reach, engagements, saves, shares, clicks, watch time, conversions)
- **Calculated rates**
- **Indexed scores and final score**
- **Notes** (anything unusual: a trend, a collaboration, a paid boost)

### Tagging Is Where the Insight Lives

The scores tell you which posts did well. The tags tell you why. Once you have a few dozen rows, use a pivot table to average the score by format, topic, hook type and day of week. Patterns appear quickly: maybe carousels consistently outperform single images for saves, or question hooks lift comments but not reach.

### Keep Boosted Posts Separate

Paid promotion inflates reach and engagement. Either exclude boosted posts from your baselines or tag them clearly and analyze them on their own.

## Weekly, Monthly or Quarterly? How Often to Review

![Review cadence compared: weekly for quick tweaks, monthly for the content mix, quarterly for strategy](/images/blog/content-performance-analysis-cadence-1200x675.webp)

| Cadence | Time needed | What to look at | Decisions it drives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | 15 to 30 minutes | Last week's posts and scores | Quick tweaks: hooks, captions, posting times |
| Monthly | 1 to 2 hours | Pivot by format, topic and hook | Next month's content mix |
| Quarterly | Half a day | Trends, goals, channel performance | Which pillars and platforms to invest in |

Weekly reviews catch obvious problems early. Monthly reviews are where the real planning happens. Quarterly reviews keep you honest about whether your content strategy still matches your business goals.

## Edge Cases: Small Accounts, Several Platforms and Viral Outliers

![Content analysis habits that work, like scoring against your median, next to habits that mislead, like judging by impressions alone](/images/blog/content-performance-analysis-mistakes-1200x675.webp)

| Problem | Fix | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fewer than a few dozen posts | Treat findings as hints to test | Patterns aren't reliable yet |
| Comparing Instagram with LinkedIn | Score each post against its own platform's baseline, then compare scores | A good rate on one network looks very different on another |
| One post went viral | Keep it out of the baseline, or rely on the median | One outlier shouldn't reset what "normal" means |
| Several changes at once | Change one thing per test | Otherwise you can't tell which change mattered |
| High impressions, no saves or clicks | Treat it as a vanity win | It rarely helps your goals |

## The Real Bottleneck: Turning Findings Into Next Month's Calendar

![The Schedule For You calendar showing a month of scheduled posts across several platforms](/images/blog/calendar-month-1440x900.webp)

Analysis is only useful if it changes what you publish. At the end of each monthly review, write down three things:

1. **Do more of:** the format, topic or hook with the highest average score.
2. **Fix or stop:** the one that consistently scores below 0.7.
3. **Test:** one new idea, with a clear hypothesis, like "a weekly myth-busting carousel will beat our tips carousels on saves."

Then put those decisions on the calendar. That's where most teams stall.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is content performance analysis?

It's measuring how your content performs against its goals, comparing posts fairly and using the findings to plan what to publish next. For social media, that usually means tracking reach, engagement, clicks and conversions per post and reviewing them on a regular schedule.

### Which metric matters most?

It depends on the post's goal. Reach and shares matter most for awareness, engagement rate and saves for community, click-through rate for traffic and conversions for sales.

### How many posts do I need before analysis is useful?

You can start straight away, but patterns become reliable once you have a few dozen posts on a platform. Until then, treat findings as hints to test.

### Should I use native analytics or a third-party tool?

Native analytics are free and usually the most detailed source for each platform. A spreadsheet that pulls those numbers together is enough for most small teams. Add other tools when manual entry starts eating too much time.

### How do I analyze content across several platforms?

Score each post against the baseline for its own platform, then compare scores rather than raw numbers. Because every score is relative, you can see which topics and formats work across channels.

### Why use the median instead of the average?

Because one viral post won't distort the median. An average that includes an outlier makes every normal post look weak.

### How long after posting should I record the numbers?

Pick one fixed window, such as seven days, and stick to it. Posts measured at different ages aren't comparable.

### Should I include boosted posts?

Only if you tag them. Paid promotion inflates reach and engagement, so exclude boosted posts from your baselines or analyze them separately.
