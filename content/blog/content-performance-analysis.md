Content performance analysis means looking at how each post performed against a goal, comparing posts fairly, and using what you learn to decide what to publish next. In practice, that's a short list of metrics tied to your goals, a consistent way to score each post, a spreadsheet you update on a set schedule, and a habit of turning findings into changes on your calendar.

You don't need a data team or expensive software, just consistency: the same metrics, measured the same way, reviewed on a regular schedule.

## The short version

1. Decide what each piece of content is for: reach, engagement, traffic or conversions.
2. Pick two or three metrics per goal and ignore the rest.
3. Normalize numbers so big and small posts can be compared.
4. Score each post with a simple, repeatable formula.
5. Tag posts by format, topic and hook so you can spot patterns.
6. Review weekly for quick fixes and monthly for bigger decisions.
7. Change something specific on next month's calendar based on what you found.

## Start with goals, not metrics

Every platform will show you dozens of numbers. Most of them don't matter for any given post. Before you open analytics, write down what the content is supposed to do.

### Awareness content

Posts meant to get in front of new people. Educational videos, shareable graphics, trend formats. You care about reach and how much of it came from people who don't follow you yet.

### Engagement content

Posts meant to start conversations or build loyalty. Questions, behind-the-scenes, community spotlights. You care about comments, replies, saves and shares relative to reach.

### Traffic content

Posts meant to send people somewhere: a blog post, a product page, a sign-up form. You care about link clicks and click-through rate, and what those visitors did next.

### Conversion content

Posts meant to drive a sale, booking or lead. You care about conversions tracked in your website analytics or store, usually through UTM-tagged links.

A single post can serve more than one goal, but give each post one primary goal. It makes scoring much simpler.

## The metrics that matter

Here's a reference table. Platform naming varies, so match these to the closest metric in each app's analytics.

| Metric | What it tells you | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | How many unique accounts saw the post | Awareness |
| Impressions / views | How many times it was shown or played | Awareness, video |
| Engagement rate | Interactions divided by reach (or impressions) | Engagement |
| Saves | People wanted to come back to it | Educational content |
| Shares / reposts | People found it worth passing on | Awareness, growth |
| Comments / replies | It sparked a conversation | Community |
| Link clicks and CTR | It drove people off-platform | Traffic |
| Average watch time / completion | People actually watched | Video |
| Follows from post | It turned viewers into followers | Growth |
| Conversions | It drove a sale, lead or sign-up | Revenue |

### Use rates, not just totals

A post that reached 50,000 people and got 500 comments did less, proportionally, than a post that reached 2,000 and got 100. Totals reward whatever got lucky with distribution. Rates tell you whether the content itself resonated. Calculate engagement rate, click-through rate and save rate so posts of different sizes can be compared.

### Be consistent about the denominator

Some teams calculate engagement rate on reach, others on impressions, others on followers. Any of these is fine as long as you use the same one every time. Write your definitions at the top of your spreadsheet so they don't drift.

### Measure at a fixed point in time

A post checked after one day and a post checked after two weeks aren't comparable. Pick a window, such as seven days after publishing, and record numbers at that point. If you can only check monthly, note the publish date so you can account for newer posts having less time.

## A simple scoring framework

Raw metrics are hard to compare across goals and platforms. A score turns each post into one number you can sort by.

### Step 1: Set a baseline for each metric

For each platform, calculate your median for the key metrics over the last 20 to 30 posts. The median is better than the average because one viral post won't distort it.

### Step 2: Index each post against the baseline

Divide each post's metric by the baseline. A post with a save rate twice your median gets 2.0 for that metric. A post at half the median gets 0.5.

### Step 3: Weight by goal

Give each metric a weight depending on the post's primary goal. An example:

| Goal | Metric weights |
|---|---|
| Awareness | Reach 50%, shares 30%, follows 20% |
| Engagement | Engagement rate 40%, comments 30%, saves 30% |
| Traffic | CTR 60%, link clicks 40% |
| Conversion | Conversions 70%, CTR 30% |

### Step 4: Calculate the score

Multiply each indexed metric by its weight and add them up. A score of 1.0 means the post performed at your typical level. Above 1.3 is a clear win worth repeating. Below 0.7 is a miss worth understanding. These thresholds are starting points; adjust them once you've seen a few months of your own data.

The point isn't mathematical precision. It's that every post gets judged the same way, so you stop arguing from gut feeling.

## A spreadsheet workflow

A plain spreadsheet works for most teams. Set up one row per post and these columns:

- **Date and time posted**
- **Platform**
- **Format** (short video, carousel, single image, text, link post, live)
- **Topic or content pillar**
- **Hook type** (question, bold claim, how-to, story, list)
- **Primary goal**
- **Raw metrics** (reach, engagements, saves, shares, clicks, watch time, conversions)
- **Calculated rates**
- **Indexed scores and final score**
- **Notes** (anything unusual: a trend, a collaboration, a paid boost)

### Tagging is where the insight lives

The scores tell you which posts did well. The tags tell you why. Once you have a few dozen rows, use a pivot table to average the score by format, by topic, by hook type and by day of week. Patterns appear quickly: maybe carousels consistently outperform single images for saves, or question hooks lift comments but not reach.

### Keep boosted posts separate

Paid promotion inflates reach and engagement. Either exclude boosted posts from your baselines or tag them clearly and analyze them on their own.

## How often to review

| Cadence | Time needed | What to look at | Decisions it drives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly | 15 to 30 minutes | Last week's posts and scores | Quick tweaks: hooks, captions, posting times |
| Monthly | 1 to 2 hours | Pivot by format, topic and hook | Next month's content mix |
| Quarterly | Half a day | Trends, goals, channel performance | Which pillars and platforms to invest in |

Weekly reviews catch obvious problems early. Monthly reviews are where the real planning happens. Quarterly reviews keep you honest about whether your content strategy still matches your business goals.

## Turning findings into the next calendar

Analysis is only useful if it changes what you publish. At the end of each monthly review, write down three things:

1. **Do more of:** the format, topic or hook with the highest average score.
2. **Fix or stop:** the one that consistently scores below 0.7.
3. **Test:** one new idea, with a clear hypothesis, like "a weekly myth-busting carousel will beat our tips carousels on saves."

Then put those decisions directly onto next month's calendar. If carousels win, schedule more carousel slots. If Tuesday mornings underperform, move those posts. If a test is running, block out enough posts to judge it fairly, usually several over a few weeks rather than one.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Chasing vanity metrics.** High impressions with no saves, clicks or follows rarely help your goals.
- **Changing too many things at once.** If you change format, topic and time together, you won't know which change mattered.
- **Comparing across platforms directly.** A good engagement rate on one network can look very different from another. Compare posts within the same platform.
- **Drawing conclusions from one post.** One viral hit or flop can be noise. Look for patterns across several posts.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is content performance analysis?

It's the process of measuring how your content performs against its goals, comparing posts fairly and using the findings to plan what to publish next. For social media, that usually means tracking reach, engagement, clicks and conversions per post and reviewing them on a regular schedule.

### Which metric matters most?

It depends on the post's goal. Reach and shares matter most for awareness, engagement rate and saves for community, click-through rate for traffic and conversions for sales. Choose a small set per goal rather than tracking everything.

### How many posts do I need before analysis is useful?

You can start reviewing straight away, but patterns become reliable once you have a few dozen posts on a platform. Until then, treat findings as hints to test rather than firm conclusions.

### Should I use native analytics or a third-party tool?

Native analytics in each platform are free and usually the most detailed source for that platform. A spreadsheet that pulls those numbers together is enough for most small teams. Add other tools when manual entry starts eating too much time.

### How do I analyze content across several platforms?

Score each post against the baseline for its own platform, then compare scores rather than raw numbers. Because every score is relative to that platform's typical performance, you can see which topics and formats work across channels.
