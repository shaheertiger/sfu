The best days to post on social media are the days your own audience is most active and most likely to engage, and they differ by platform. Work-focused networks like LinkedIn tend to suit weekdays, while entertainment and inspiration platforms like TikTok, Instagram and Pinterest can do well on weekends too. Start with a sensible hypothesis for each platform, then test it against your own analytics for a few weeks.

You'll find plenty of charts online claiming one universal "best day." The problem is that they average together millions of very different accounts. Your audience, your time zone and your content type matter more than any average. This guide gives you reasoned starting points and a method to replace them with your own data.

## Starting days to test, by platform

These are starting hypotheses based on how people typically use each platform, not findings from a study. Use them to decide what to test first, then let your own results overrule them.

| Platform | Days to test first | Reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LinkedIn | Tuesday to Thursday | People use it around work; Mondays are busy and Fridays wind down |
| Facebook | Weekdays, plus one weekend day | Broad, mixed audience that checks in throughout the week |
| Instagram | Midweek and Sunday | Leisure scrolling happens on weekdays and weekends |
| TikTok | Thursday to Sunday | Entertainment viewing often increases toward the weekend |
| X | Monday to Thursday | News and conversation follow the workweek |
| Threads and Bluesky | Weekdays | Conversation-led feeds that reward joining in when people are around |
| Pinterest | Weekend and Sunday evening | People plan meals, projects and purchases in their free time |
| YouTube | Publish Thursday or Friday | Gives videos time to gather views before weekend viewing |
| Google Business Profile | A day or two before your busiest days | Customers check before they visit |

Treat this as a first draft. A B2B brand on Instagram and a meme account on LinkedIn would both break these patterns, and that's fine.

## Why the best day differs by platform

The underlying question is simple: on which days do your followers have the time and the mindset to engage with what you post? Each platform answers that differently.

### LinkedIn: tied to the workweek

LinkedIn is used mostly in a professional context. Many people check it during work hours, during breaks or on the way to work. That makes weekdays the natural place to start. Mondays can be crowded with catching up, and Friday afternoons often see people switching off, which is why midweek is a common first test. If your audience is freelancers or founders, weekends may be worth testing too.

### Facebook: broad and steady

Facebook audiences span ages, interests and routines. Many people check in briefly every day rather than in a burst. That makes Facebook less dependent on any single day. Local businesses and community Pages often find that posting before the weekend helps, because people are making plans. For hour-by-hour starting points, see the [best time to post on Facebook](/blog/best-time-to-post-on-facebook).

### Instagram: work breaks and downtime

Instagram is used for leisure, but people scroll at lunch and on commutes as well as on weekends. Test weekdays against weekends. If you'd like to go deeper on the start of the week specifically, our guide to the [best time to post on Instagram on Monday](/blog/best-time-to-post-on-instagram-on-monday) shows how to read your follower activity hour by hour.

### TikTok: entertainment first

TikTok is built around entertainment and discovery. Since the For You feed recommends videos to people who don't follow you, a post can keep finding viewers for days. The day you post still matters for the first wave of views, and evenings and weekends, when people have time to watch, are worth testing. Our guide to the [best time to post on TikTok](/blog/best-time-to-post-on-tiktok) gives a starting time for each day.

### X, Threads and Bluesky: conversation in real time

These feeds reward joining conversations while they're happening. News, industry chatter and live events drive activity, and many of those follow the workweek. The right day may also depend on events in your niche: a product launch, a conference or a sports fixture can matter more than the day of the week.

### Pinterest: planning ahead

People use Pinterest to plan: recipes for the week, home projects, outfits, gifts and events. Planning often happens in free time. Pinterest content also has a long life and is strongly seasonal, so publishing seasonal pins well ahead of the event can matter more than the exact day.

### YouTube: publish before people watch

YouTube viewing often increases when people have more free time, such as evenings and weekends. Many creators publish a day or two before those peaks so the video has time to be picked up by subscribers and recommendations. Consistency also matters: a regular publishing day helps subscribers know when to expect new videos.

### Google Business Profile: think like a customer

Posts on your Business Profile are seen by people searching for you or for businesses like yours. Post offers and updates shortly before your busiest days. A restaurant might post on Thursday for the weekend, while a B2B service might post at the start of the week.

## How to find your own best days

Your analytics will tell you more than any table. Here's a simple process that works on any platform.

### Step 1: Check what your analytics already show

1. Open the native analytics for each platform: Instagram Insights, Facebook and Instagram data in Meta Business Suite, LinkedIn Page analytics, TikTok Studio analytics, YouTube Studio analytics, Pinterest Analytics and so on.
2. Look for an audience activity view, if there is one. Instagram, for example, shows your followers' most active times by day and hour. If you don't have Insights yet, our guide on [how to turn on Insights on Instagram](/blog/how-to-turn-on-insights-on-instagram) explains how.
3. Export or note your last two or three months of posts with the day each was published and its reach and engagement.

### Step 2: Form a hypothesis

Combine the audience activity data with the starting points above. For example: "Our LinkedIn posts will get more engagement on Tuesday and Wednesday than on Monday and Friday."

### Step 3: Run a fair test

1. **Keep content comparable.** Rotate the same type of post across different days. Don't put your best video on Tuesday and a plain link on Friday.
2. **Keep the time of day the same** while you're testing days, so you're only changing one thing.
3. **Test for at least four weeks.** One week can be distorted by a holiday, a news event or one unusually strong post.
4. **Measure after the same time window**, such as 48 hours or 7 days after posting, so every post has the same chance.

### Step 4: Compare the right metrics

- **Reach or impressions** show whether people saw the post.
- **Engagement rate** (interactions divided by reach) lets you compare posts with different reach.
- **Clicks** matter if your goal is traffic.
- **Saves and shares** are useful signs that content was valuable.
- **Follows or subscribers gained** show whether a post brought in new people.

For a deeper framework on comparing posts fairly, see our guide to [content performance analysis](/blog/content-performance-analysis).

### Step 5: Decide, then retest

Pick the days that are consistently strong, not the one with a single big hit. Build your schedule around them, and retest every quarter or when your audience changes, for example after growing into a new country or shifting your content focus.

## Common mistakes when choosing posting days

- **Copying another account's schedule.** Their audience isn't yours.
- **Testing too many things at once.** If you change the day, time, format and topic together, you won't know what made the difference.
- **Ignoring time zones.** A Tuesday post at 9 a.m. in New York is Tuesday afternoon in London and late Tuesday night in Sydney.
- **Going quiet on "bad" days.** A lower-performing day can still be worth posting on if you have good content. The aim is to put your most important posts on your strongest days, not to stop posting.
- **Stopping after one test.** Audiences and platforms change. What worked last year may not work now.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the single best day to post on social media?

There isn't one that works for everyone. The best day depends on the platform, your audience's routines and their time zones. Use the starting points in this guide as hypotheses and test them with your own analytics.

### Is it bad to post on weekends?

Not necessarily. Platforms built around entertainment, inspiration and planning, like TikTok, Instagram and Pinterest, can perform well on weekends. Work-focused platforms like LinkedIn often see less activity, but that depends on your audience.

### How long should I test posting days before deciding?

At least four weeks, keeping the content type and time of day consistent. Longer is better if you post infrequently, because you need several posts on each day to see a reliable pattern.

### Should I post on the same days on every platform?

Not usually. Each platform's audience behaves differently, so your best LinkedIn day may be a weak Pinterest day. Plan a separate schedule for each platform based on its own data.

### Does posting on the best day matter more than posting consistently?

Consistency usually matters more. A regular schedule your audience can rely on, with strong content, will generally do better than chasing the perfect day with irregular posts. Use your best days to decide where your most important posts go.
